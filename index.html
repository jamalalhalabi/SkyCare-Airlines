01 · The Problem

Flying Shouldn’t Be the Most Stressful Part of Traveling.

Most airline frustration is not caused by the flight itself. It comes from how passengers are treated when something changes.

Ask the room: Raise your hand if you have ever stood in a rebooking line after a delay or cancellation.

  • Poor communication
  • Long customer service lines
  • Confusing disruption procedures
  • Lack of transparency
  • Hard to reach a real person
  • Baggage uncertainty
  • Repeating the story to every agent
  • Inconsistent service recovery

The Typical Travel Experience

  1. Delay
  2. Little information
  3. Passenger confusion
  4. Long line
  5. Repeated explanations
  6. Frustration

The SkyCare Model

  1. Issue detected
  2. Passenger notified
  3. Cause explained
  4. Options provided
  5. Rebooking assisted
  6. Follow-up completed

Customers do not expect airlines to control everything. They expect airlines to communicate clearly, respect their time, and take responsibility when problems occur.

    We Can’t Control the Weather. We Can Control How We Respond.

    Always in SkyCare’s control

    • How fast we tell you
    • How clearly we explain why
    • Which options we offer

    • Whether we follow up

    SkyCare Service Commitments (concept targets for this proposal)

    • 15minDisruption alert after a schedule change is confirmed
    • 1agentOne person owns your case until it is solved
    • 0linesRebooking options reach your phone before you reach a counter
    • 24hrsPersonal follow-up after every disruption

    Regulations are the floor, not the goal

    U.S. DOT rules require refunds when a flight is canceled or significantly changed (for example, a domestic schedule change of more than 3 hours) and a refund of bag fees when checked bags are significantly delayed (U.S. Department of Transportation, 2024). SkyCare treats these as the minimum and acts before the passenger has to ask.

    How we measure success

    • Time to first alert
    • First-contact resolution
    • Rebooking acceptance
    • Recovery satisfaction
    • Repeat bookings

    Service pays for itself: satisfaction and perceived value drive repeat travel (Chen, 2008; Park et al., 2004).

    11 · Research Behind the SkyCare Experience

    Built on Evidence, Not Guesswork

    Five peer-reviewed studies of air passengers, including a field study conducted during real flight delays.

    Service quality & loyalty

    Finding: Service value, passenger satisfaction, and airline image each directly affected whether passengers intended to fly the airline again (Park et al., 2004).

    SkyCare applies it: service is built into every stage of the journey, not one department.

    Perceived value

    Finding: Perceived value and satisfaction both drove future intentions, and perceived value had the larger effect (Chen, 2008).

    SkyCare applies it: clear pricing and real help when plans change make the trip feel worth the fare.

    Service recovery

    Finding: Fair outcomes, fair procedures, and respectful treatment all shaped satisfaction with airline recovery; the authors concluded compensation beyond expectations is likely the most effective strategy (Migacz et al., 2018).

    SkyCare applies it: a fair fix, a fast process, and respectful communication, then follow-up.

    Flight delays

    Finding: Among passengers surveyed during real delays, justice and emotions shaped recovery satisfaction, which led to trust, repurchase, and word of mouth (Wen & Chi, 2013).

    SkyCare applies it: the delay response manages emotions, not just logistics.

    Self-service technology

    Finding: Online and kiosk check-in are growing, but frequent flyers preferred self check-in while business passengers preferred the desk (Castillo-Manzano & López-Valpuesta, 2013).

    SkyCare applies it: passengers choose the channel; no one is forced into an app.
